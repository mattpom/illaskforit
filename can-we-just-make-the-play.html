Can We Just Make the Play and Move On?

Does every routine play in the NFL need a choreographed celebration? Make the play, get back in the huddle and move on.

Words by Sean Matthews · Sports · One Man's Opinion

I have a question for today’s NFL players: Does every routine play require a Broadway production afterward?

A defensive back breaks up a pass on second down and immediately signals incomplete as though he just personally saved the franchise. A linebacker tackles a running back after a three-yard gain, then runs fifteen yards downfield to pose with six teammates. A receiver catches a pass for a first down and performs an elaborate routine that apparently required rehearsals, costumes and a choreographer.

Congratulations. You did the thing listed in your job description.

I understand celebrating a touchdown. Interception? Absolutely. Game-winning field goal? Lose your mind. If you make a genuinely spectacular play at a critical moment, enjoy it. Football is emotional, and nobody wants players behaving like they are clocking in for a shift at an accounting firm.

But we have completely lost the plot.

I just watched three Atlanta Falcons sack Aaron Rodgers at the end of the half. Instead of simply getting up and leaving the field, all three sat down together in some kind of choreographed praying pose.

Why?

You made a sack. It was a good play. The half was over. Walk to the locker room.

But apparently even that required a group performance. Ridiculous.

Now, every ordinary tackle is treated like a goal-line stand in the Super Bowl. Every incomplete pass triggers finger-wagging, chest-pounding and enough posing to fill a family photo album. Half the time, the celebrating team is losing by two touchdowns.

Maybe check the scoreboard before unveiling the dance routine.

The group celebrations are particularly exhausting. One player makes a decent play, and suddenly the entire defense must sprint to the end zone to perform a skit. Players pretend to row a boat, shoot imaginary arrows, take fake photographs or participate in some other carefully planned production.

Meanwhile, the rest of us are waiting for the football game to resume.

When exactly are these routines rehearsed? Is practice divided into tackling drills, pass coverage and theatrical blocking? Does an assistant coach stand nearby holding a clipboard and say, “The defense looked sharp today, but we need more commitment during the imaginary bowling celebration”?

The funniest part is what happens when the replay begins.

A defender jumps up and celebrates a massive hit, only for the replay to show that the runner gained eight yards and achieved a first down. A defensive back struts around after an incompletion, even though the quarterback simply overthrew an open receiver by five yards. A linebacker celebrates a sack while a penalty flag sits directly behind him.

Nothing kills a celebration faster than hearing, “Holding, defense. Automatic first down.”

“You made the play. Good. Now get back in the huddle.”

Sean Matthews · Sports

This is not an argument against personality. The NFL should be entertaining. Players should have fun. Fans enjoy emotion, rivalries and moments of genuine spontaneity.

The problem is that none of this feels spontaneous anymore.

It feels mandatory.

Make any play—regardless of how common or inconsequential—and immediately begin performing. Find the nearest camera. Summon your teammates. Strike the pose. Make sure the moment can be clipped, posted and turned into content before the next commercial break.

Perhaps that is the real issue. Players are not merely playing football anymore. They are also managing personal brands, chasing highlights and competing for attention. A routine tackle might disappear from memory, but a ridiculous celebration could circulate online all week.

Still, there is something refreshing about the player who makes a big play, hands the ball to the official and gets ready for the next snap. It projects confidence. It says, “Of course I made the play. That is what I am here to do.”

You know what is more intimidating than eleven defenders sprinting to the end zone for a choreographed photo?

A defender knocking you backward, saying nothing and lining up to do it again.

Celebrate the moments that deserve celebrating. Enjoy the touchdowns, turnovers, game-winners and genuinely extraordinary plays. Football would be painfully dull without emotion.

But after a routine tackle on second-and-seven?

Get back in the huddle. There is another play coming.

↑ The Verdict

MAKE THE PLAY. MOVE ON.

Save the production for a moment that actually deserves one. A routine tackle, pass breakup or sack does not require a cast, a rehearsal and a camera-ready pose.
