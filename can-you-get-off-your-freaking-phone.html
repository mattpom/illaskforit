Can You Get Off Your Freaking Phone?

When you work with the public, noticing the public is part of the job.

Words by Sean Matthews · One Man's Opinion

I went to my eye doctor today. Nothing unusual about that. What was unusual was standing at the counter waiting to be helped while the person behind it stared at their phone and did not even notice I was there.

I was not across the room. I was not hiding behind a plant. I was standing at the counter—the exact place where patients go when they need assistance.

Still, the phone won.

Later, I went to a restaurant and saw the same problem in a different uniform. The waiters were sitting around looking at their phones while customers were waiting. Nobody appeared especially concerned that people had entered the restaurant expecting service.

This is not an attack on phones. We all use them. They help us work, communicate, navigate, pay bills, take pictures and waste ridiculous amounts of time. The problem is not owning a phone. The problem is forgetting that the person standing in front of you should matter more than whatever is happening on the screen.

When you are working with the public, noticing the public is part of the job.

Customers should not have to wave, cough, clear their throats or perform a small stage show just to get an employee's attention. A patient standing at a medical counter should not feel invisible. A restaurant customer should not have to compete with a server's text messages, social-media feed or group chat.

“When you work with the public, noticing the public is part of the job.”

Sean Matthews · One Man's Opinion

And yes, I understand that some employees use phones for work. Restaurants may use them to check schedules, communicate about orders or handle other business. Medical offices may use mobile devices for legitimate reasons too. But from the customer's side of the counter, there is an easy way to tell when something has gone wrong: nobody acknowledges you.

A simple “I'll be right with you” changes the entire situation. It takes two seconds. It tells the customer, “I see you. You matter. I have not forgotten why I am here.”

That is the part that seems to be disappearing—the basic awareness that another human being is waiting.

Good service does not require a grand performance. Look up. Make eye contact. Say hello. If you must finish something, acknowledge the person and give them a realistic idea of how long it will take. Then put the phone down and do the job.

Phones are useful tools. They should not become shields employees hide behind while customers stand there wondering whether they are invisible.

So, on behalf of everyone who has waited at a counter while an employee scrolled, typed or stared at a screen, I have one simple question:

Can you get off your freaking phone?
