Prayer, the Pledge, and the End of Critical Thinking

What did we expect children to learn from the words, and what are we teaching them now?

By Sean Matthews · November 6, 2026

The most interesting kid in a classroom saying the Pledge of Allegiance might be the one who asks what “liberty and justice for all” actually means. A good teacher could spend the rest of the period on that question. A teacher in a hurry could tell the kid to put his hand over his heart and get on with it. Same pledge, two very different educations.

That’s what gets lost when people argue about prayer and the Pledge in public schools. We spend all our time fighting over whether the words should be said. We spend a lot less time asking what children were supposed to think about once they’d said them.

The history matters. In West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette in 1943, the Supreme Court ruled that public schools couldn’t compel students to salute the flag and recite the Pledge. In Engel v. Vitale in 1962, the Court struck down a New York public school’s use of a state-composed prayer, even though students could be excused. Neither ruling imposed a nationwide ban on the Pledge or on students’ private prayer. Schools may hold Pledge exercises, with students’ freedom of conscience protected. Students may pray privately, subject to ordinary rules against disrupting instruction. Sources below.

I see those fights as part of a larger argument about what schools are for. My concern is that, wherever shared routines weakened, the opportunity to teach their underlying ideas could weaken with them. That is an interpretation, and these court decisions do not establish that changes to prayer or the Pledge caused a decline in critical thinking. The question worth asking is what schools do to teach responsibility, citizenship and independent judgment.

When an old routine falls away or becomes something we rush through, there’s a decision to make about what to do with the time and attention it once commanded. We could use that opening to get more serious about what we owe each other, what citizenship asks of us, and how to live with people who believe differently. Those questions are worth a few minutes of the school day.

For some people, a morning prayer was a moment to be grateful and remember that they weren’t the center of the universe. For others, it could be a reminder that the school assumed everybody in the room believed the same thing. Both experiences belong in the conversation. Put them side by side and you have a real question for a classroom. How do you make room for someone’s faith when the person sitting next to them has a different faith or none at all?

The Pledge offers an equally good lesson. Ask one student why participating matters to her. Ask another why he chooses to decline. Then ask each of them to explain the other’s reasoning in a way the other would recognize. That exercise takes more patience than reciting the Pledge. It also teaches a skill they’ll need every time they have a serious disagreement as adults.

Look at how easy it is to argue now. A clip hits your phone. It starts halfway through an argument. You know who posted it, you know which side you’re supposed to be on, and the button to share it is right there. The useful questions take longer. What happened before the clip started? Would I find this convincing if it came from somebody I can’t stand? What would make me change my mind?

The ability to pause, consider what you owe other people, and question your first reaction has to be practiced somewhere. A classroom is one of the few places where a kid can try out an argument, hear it challenged, and get another shot at making it. That opportunity disappears whenever the adults in the room treat an honest question as a disruption.

If a school says the Pledge tomorrow morning, give the class five minutes afterward to talk about one line. Start with “for all.” Ask the kids to name a time America lived up to those words and a time it fell short. Make them explain how they decided. And when a student gives an answer that makes the room uncomfortable, give that student enough time to finish.
