The Best Right Fielder Ever—And Why This Statue Still Matters to Pittsburgh

Why Roberto Clemente deserves to be called baseball’s greatest right fielder—and why his statue outside PNC Park still matters to Pittsburgh.

Words by Sean Matthews · Deeper Dive · Sports

Outside PNC Park, Roberto Clemente is frozen at the moment he stops being a hitter and becomes a runner.

His bat has dropped behind him. His body is already moving toward first base. Beyond him stands the yellow bridge that carries his name, tying the ballpark to downtown Pittsburgh.

This is not simply another statue of a former athlete.

It is Pittsburgh’s monument to the greatest right fielder who ever played the game—and to a man whose importance became even greater once the uniform came off.

The Greatest Right Fielder Ever

Baseball arguments are supposed to be complicated. This one does not need to be.

Roberto Clemente played 18 Major League seasons, all with the Pittsburgh Pirates. He finished with exactly 3,000 hits, a .317 career batting average, four National League batting titles, 12 consecutive Gold Gloves, 15 All-Star selections, two World Series championships and the 1966 National League Most Valuable Player Award.

Those numbers alone place him among the greatest players in baseball history.

Then there was the way he played right field.

Clemente possessed one of the strongest and most accurate throwing arms the sport has ever seen. Runners had to reconsider taking an extra base because Clemente could retrieve a ball near the wall and deliver it to third base with astonishing speed and precision.

He did not merely occupy right field. He controlled it.

Other right fielders produced more home runs. Some accumulated higher modern offensive statistics. Hank Aaron was a greater power hitter. Tony Gwynn became one of the purest hitters the game has known. Al Kaline, Frank Robinson and Ichiro Suzuki all belong in the conversation.

But the complete package matters.

Clemente combined elite hitting, extraordinary defense, speed, throwing ability, postseason performance, longevity and an unmistakable competitive presence. He could change a game with his bat, glove, legs or arm.

If the assignment is to choose one man to play right field, Roberto Clemente is the choice.

Exactly 3,000

On September 30, 1972, Clemente stepped to the plate at Three Rivers Stadium with 2,999 career hits.

Facing New York Mets pitcher Jon Matlack, he drove a double into left-center field. It was hit number 3,000, making Clemente the first Latin American player to reach the milestone.

It was also his final regular-season hit.

There is something almost impossible about the precision of that ending. Not 3,001. Not 3,010. Exactly 3,000—one of baseball’s most important numbers reached in the final regular-season game he would ever play.

Clemente appeared in the National League Championship Series that October, but he would never return for the 1973 season.

More Than a Baseball Story

Clemente was born in Carolina, Puerto Rico, and carried enormous pride in where he came from.

That pride mattered during an era when Latin American players were frequently stereotyped, misquoted or treated as outsiders by the American baseball establishment. Reporters sometimes mocked Clemente’s accent or altered his words when quoting him.

Clemente pushed back.

He insisted that journalists represent him accurately. He spoke about the racism experienced by Black and Latino players. He refused to make himself smaller or more agreeable simply to satisfy people who were uncomfortable with his confidence.

That made him more than a baseball star. It made him an important figure for generations of Latin American players who followed him.

Clemente proved that a Puerto Rican player could become one of baseball’s defining figures without surrendering his identity to do it.

The Mission That Cost Him His Life

On December 23, 1972, a devastating earthquake struck Managua, Nicaragua.

Clemente helped organize shipments of food, medicine and other relief supplies. After learning that earlier aid might not be reaching the earthquake victims, he decided to accompany another shipment personally.

On New Year’s Eve, the overloaded cargo plane carrying Clemente and the relief supplies crashed into the Atlantic Ocean shortly after taking off from Puerto Rico.

Clemente was 38 years old. His body was never recovered.

His death is often described as the moment he became a humanitarian symbol, but that misses an essential part of the story. Clemente had already spent years helping people.

He organized free baseball clinics for children in Puerto Rico. He visited young patients in Pittsburgh hospitals, often without alerting reporters. He used his money, fame and time to help people who could do nothing for his career in return.

The flight to Nicaragua was not an unexpected act of heroism. It was consistent with the way Clemente had chosen to live.

Why the Statue Still Matters

Sculptor Susan Wagner’s 12-foot bronze statue of Clemente was dedicated in 1994 outside Three Rivers Stadium. It was later moved to its current location near PNC Park’s center-field entrance and the Roberto Clemente Bridge.

The monument’s base is not an ordinary pedestal.

It contains a baseball diamond surrounded by 15 inscriptions tracing Clemente’s life. Beneath glass representations of the bases is soil from Puerto Rico, Forbes Field and Three Rivers Stadium—three pieces of ground that formed his journey.

Space was deliberately left for a 16th inscription. It remains empty, symbolizing the unfinished circle of a life cut short.

That detail changes the entire monument.

The statue does not only celebrate what Clemente accomplished. It asks visitors to consider what else he might have done.

He might have continued his humanitarian work. He might have become a manager, baseball executive or public servant. He might have spent decades advocating for Puerto Rico and Latin American athletes.

The empty space represents all of it.

Pittsburgh’s Number 21

Clemente’s number 21 has been retired by the Pirates. The bridge beside PNC Park bears his name. The ballpark’s right-field wall rises 21 feet in his honor.

But Clemente’s connection to Pittsburgh goes deeper than official tributes.

Pittsburgh respects people who work hard, speak directly and do not need attention for doing the right thing. Clemente embodied those qualities. He was graceful but never soft, proud but never hollow, and generous without turning generosity into a performance.

He belonged first to Puerto Rico, but Pittsburgh understood him.

That is why people still stop at the statue. It is why flowers, flags and personal messages appear near it. It is why number 21 remains different from every other number in Pirates history.

The city is not merely remembering a player.

It is remembering its standard.

Still Moving

The statue does not show Clemente posing or admiring a hit. It catches him dropping the bat and accelerating toward first base.

He is moving forward.

That is how Roberto Clemente should be remembered—not as a distant figure sealed inside baseball history, but as an example still challenging the people who pass him.

Play the game completely. Defend who you are. Use whatever success you earn to help someone else.

That is why Roberto Clemente was the greatest right fielder ever.

And that is why his statue still matters to Pittsburgh.
