The Case For Doing Absolutely Nothing This Summer

No plans. No itinerary. Just a porch, a hammock, and permission to waste an afternoon. Turns out that might be the smartest thing you do all summer.

I'll Ask For It · Deeper Dive · One Man's Opinion

There's a version of me that used to feel guilty about doing nothing. I couldn't tell you exactly when that started, but somewhere along the way, an empty Saturday afternoon stopped feeling like rest and started feeling like a personal failure. Nothing on the calendar. No trip booked. No project moving forward. Just — nothing.

Then I found out I'd been wrong to feel guilty about it, and I had the receipts to prove it.

Every summer comes loaded with an unspoken assignment: make it count. Fill it with cookouts, road trips, lake days, concerts, something with a photo at the end of it. If July ends and you can't point to a highlight reel, it can feel like the season got away from you.

I'd like to make the case for the opposite. A slow, undocumented, mostly boring afternoon with nothing on it might be the single best thing you do for yourself all summer.

Here's why I actually believe that, and not just because I'd rather sit on a porch than plan a trip.

Researchers publishing through the American Psychological Association looked at how people's sense of well-being changes as their free time increases. Well-being went up as free time increased, but only to a point, leveling off at around two hours a day and starting to slide by around five. People who imagined having a wide-open, unstructured amount of time every day for months actually reported feeling worse than people who imagined having a modest, comfortable amount. Too much unstructured time left them feeling unproductive, and that feeling dragged their mood down right along with it.

In other words: a lazy afternoon is good for you. An entire lazy season, with nothing to push against, isn't automatically better. The sweet spot is smaller and more specific than "as much free time as possible."

That tracks with something else I noticed: most people aren't even using the free time they're already owed. Pew Research Center found that 46 percent of U.S. workers who get paid time off from their job take less of it than their employer actually offers. Not because the time isn't there. Because people talk themselves out of using it, feeling like they don't need it, or worrying about what piles up at work while they're gone.

So the problem isn't really a shortage of hours. It's that most of us have been trained to treat unstructured time as something to feel guilty about, or something that has to be "earned" with a trip elaborate enough to justify the days off.

I don't think it needs to be earned. A hammock in the backyard on a Tuesday afternoon, with the phone left inside, counts for just as much as a flight somewhere. The lake day works. The porch works just as well. The point was never the location. It was the absence of an agenda.

None of this means doing nothing all summer, every summer. That's not what the research says, and it's not what I'm arguing either. It means protecting a few actual, unscheduled hours on purpose, instead of hoping they show up between errands.

So that's my case. Not for a big trip. Not for an ambitious summer bucket list. Just for one boring, deliberately empty afternoon, sometime soon, with absolutely nothing to show for it afterward.

What would you even do with an afternoon like that?
